# Conclusion

AppShield is designed around a sound idea: compare what a server looks like now with a record of how it looked when it was trusted, and when the server itself can no longer be believed, examine it from outside. The threat model shows that the idea holds, but that the design as described trusts several things it has no means of checking.

In normal mode, the three weaknesses that dominate the ranking are the unprotected channel between console and host, the unprotected files that hold the baseline and configuration, and a console login with no stated limits or roles. None of them needs a new invention to fix. Mutual TLS, signed and versioned storage, slow password hashing with a second factor, and separate roles are well understood and standardised, and together they fully mitigate eight of the sixteen highest ranked threats and reduce the rest.

The bootable mode removes the network and the possibly compromised operating system from the picture, and with them the threats a rootkit poses to the normal mode. It does not remove trust; it moves it onto the boot media, the firmware that loads it, the baseline carried with it and the drive that brings the results back. Those threats rank lower because each one concerns a single machine and needs physical access, but they are the last line of defence, so they deserve procedures of their own (M11). One threat remains out of reach: hardware below the trusted environment that returns false data. That risk has to be accepted and documented.

Using pytm alongside the manual STRIDE analysis worked well. Writing the diagrams as code kept the six DFDs, their flow tables and the tool findings consistent with each other, and the tool's library was thorough on generic weaknesses. The threats that matter most for an integrity checker, however, came from thinking about what the data means: a baseline accepted without review, an old baseline restored, a hash collision chosen to keep a change hidden. A tool can suggest where to look, but the analysis still has to be done by people who understand what the system is for.

Several points remain open because the brief does not settle them, chiefly the protocol between console and host, how credentials are stored, and who approves a new baseline. Each was handled here with the less favourable assumption. The model should be revisited once those decisions are made, and the pytm models and threat register in the repository make that a matter of updating the data and rebuilding the report.
